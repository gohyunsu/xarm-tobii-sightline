Robot teleoperation is
an evidence-management problem.

This page builds the study from the ground up: what xArm contributes, why camera geometry changes near contact, what a screen-based Tobii tracker actually measures, how gaze becomes an uncertainty-aware AOI observation, and why an adaptive interface can help or harm.

1 · Teleoperation

The operator acts through a mediated loop

In direct manipulation, the person can move their head, use binocular depth, feel unexpected contact, and look around an occluder. Screen-based teleoperation compresses that rich loop into video, interface state, and controller feedback.

Observe

Cameras sample the workcell from predetermined locations. Lens, frame rate, exposure, calibration, latency, and occlusion determine what evidence reaches the operator.

Interpret

The operator infers target identity, depth, gap, alignment, and whether the previous command had the intended effect. This inference is not visible in robot telemetry alone.

Act

The controller produces an explicit motion request. Guards constrain it before xArm executes. The resulting state and new images close the loop.

Why a camera interface is part of the control system

The camera does not exert force, yet it changes the information on which every force-producing decision depends. A layout can therefore improve or degrade control without changing the robot policy. This is why the study holds controller mapping, speed, and camera streams constant while manipulating only the display policy.

Latency has several locations

Camera exposure, encoding, transport, rendering, gaze callbacks, adaptation logic, ROS communication, and robot dynamics each add delay. End-to-end event timing matters more than quoting a single device rate. The experiment records source timestamps and verifies residual offset with observable shared events.

2 · Robot platform

xArm is the physical testbed—not the research claim

The study uses a compact tabletop manipulator because self-occlusion, depth ambiguity, and contact alignment can be reproduced safely with lightweight objects. Exact specifications remain provisional until the actual inventory is audited.

Degrees of freedom

xArm5/6/7 have different kinematic redundancy and occlusion profiles. “xArm” is not one interchangeable geometry.

End effector

Gripper finger width, camera mount, and payload change both the visible contact region and collision envelope.

Control mode

Joint, Cartesian pose, and Cartesian velocity modes create different latency and guard requirements. The selected mode must be frozen across conditions.

Reference only

xArm6 official figures—6 DoF, 700 mm reach, 5 kg maximum payload, ±0.1 mm repeatability—apply only if xArm6 is the audited device.

Implementation path: the official xarm_ros2 repository ↗ includes ROS 2, MoveIt, D435i eye-in-hand, hand–eye calibration, and vision-guided grasping examples. Example calibration values are never reused as experimental calibration.

3 · Camera geometry

Each view answers a different question

A useful interface does not ask which camera is “best” in general. It asks which observable is required in the current task phase, and what each view cannot reliably reveal.

Overhead / worldTarget identity, global path, XY alignment, destinationZ-gap, contact edge, gripper self-occlusionSearch, approach, transport
SideHeight, surface separation, stack level, insertion depthXY overlap and objects hidden behind one anotherPre-contact, placement, verification
Wrist / eye-in-handLocal gripper–object relation and close contact detailNarrow context, motion-induced image change, arm occlusionFinal approach, grasp, insertion

Occlusion is phase-dependent

Visibility must be computed for the critical object surface, not the object’s full bounding box. An object can remain “detected” while the contact edge is completely hidden.

Mapping is a confound

If side-view axes are rotated relative to controller axes, performance may change because of visuomotor remapping rather than selective assistance.

Motion is a cue and a cost

A moving camera can reveal depth through motion parallax but can also disturb spatial orientation. The MVP selects among fixed streams before considering motorized viewpoints.

4 · Screen-based eye tracking

Tobii estimates a point of gaze on the display

A screen-based tracker illuminates and observes the eyes, estimates an eye model, and maps gaze to display coordinates through calibration. The output is a noisy time series—not a direct recording of thoughts.

Illuminate and image

Near-infrared illuminators create corneal reflections while cameras observe the pupil and eye. Pupil-center corneal-reflection geometry supports gaze estimation.

Calibrate to the current screen

Known display targets map eye features to screen coordinates. A successful calibration is not permanent; posture and mounting can create drift.

Validate and preserve quality

Record validation error, tracking ratio, display geometry, calibration version, and invalid samples. Never silently interpolate long gaps into apparent inspection.

Map samples to rendered content

The UI must version the exact layout and camera frame. A normalized gaze coordinate is meaningless without knowing which panel and AOI occupied that pixel at that time.

Device assumption, not inventory: Tobii Eye Tracker 5 is officially specified at 133 Hz and supports stated screen sizes on Windows 10/11. Those figures do not establish research permission, actual accuracy, or valid-sample rate. Official ET5 specifications ↗

5 · Gaze measures

A sample, fixation, transition, and scanpath answer different questions

The study avoids one-number eye tracking. It aligns visual behavior to task events and distinguishes measurement quality from behavioral interpretation.

Gaze sample

A timestamped x/y estimate plus validity and, where available, eye origin or pupil information. This is the closest level to the sensor.

Fixation

A filtered interval of relatively stable gaze. The classification depends on algorithm and threshold; it is not simply “attention happened.”

Transition

A movement between camera panels or task AOIs. Transition count and timing can expose visual-search cost hidden by total dwell.

Event-aligned sequence

The order of target, gripper, contact edge, and destination inspection relative to robot events is often more meaningful than aggregate heatmaps.

6 · Areas of interest

AOIs come from a task graph, not convenient rectangles

A whole-object box is usually too coarse. During approach, the target centroid may matter; during pre-contact, the contact edge and z-gap matter; during transport, object retention and destination preview matter.

Dynamic geometry

Robot, object, and rendered-panel geometry update AOI polygons by frame. A version identifier allows later recomputation.

Visibility state

An AOI can exist but be partly hidden. Coverage and visibility are recorded separately so looking at an occluded region is not treated as seeing its evidence.

Manual quality audit

A stratified frame sample is checked for AOI placement and occlusion labels. Detector output is not accepted as ground truth without audit.

7 · Adaptive interfaces

An assist can become a new disturbance

Every automatic screen change is also a visual event. It can capture gaze, break spatial memory, induce a corrective hand motion, and teach the operator to wait for the interface.

Midas touch

Looking is not selecting. Sightline does not switch on every fixation or use gaze as a click.

Camera churn

Threshold crossings can cause flicker. Minimum on-time, separate release threshold, and cooldown create hysteresis.

Attentional capture

The expansion itself attracts gaze. C2 and C3 use identical size, placement, color, and animation so trigger information is the only difference.

Automation bias

No cue is not an all-clear. The UI avoids green approval symbols and retains operator responsibility.

Design constraints derived from those risks

  • Keep the main camera and controller mapping stable.
  • Place each auxiliary feed in a consistent screen location.
  • Limit the display to one auxiliary view at a time in the MVP.
  • Expose explicit close, pin, and manual-view controls.
  • Hide the live gaze cursor during trials to avoid changing natural looking behavior.
  • Log every cue, ignored cue, override, and view transition as an event.

8 · Legal, ethical, and safety boundaries

Technical access is not research permission

Before code is treated as a study instrument, the team must verify the exact device, SDK rights, human-data governance, and independent robot safety controls.

Tobii analytical use

Tobii states that storing or transferring gaze to analyze, record, or visualize behavior can require an Analytical Use License under the Stream Data License Agreement. Obtain written confirmation for the exact SKU and use.

Official SDLA guidance ↗

Human-data governance

Define consent, withdrawal, pseudonymous IDs, access control, raw-video/gaze retention, and what derived data may be released. Avoid collecting face video unless a justified measure requires it.

Robot safety remains separate

Use lightweight props, low speed and acceleration, workspace limits, a barrier, dead-man control, physical E-stop, and a recovery checklist. The gaze assistant is never a protective function.

Gate rule: without verified analytical-use permission, sufficient end-to-end synchronization, institutional ethics determination, and independent workcell controls, the project stops at non-human technical testing or offline shadow-mode analysis.
